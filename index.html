Bank math

A power bank is sold by its biggest number: the milliamp-hours, printed large, counted at the cell's own voltage. The honesty comes in three moves. First the milliamp-hours become watt-hours, the one number the label and the airline both speak. Then the watt-hours become charges, after the conversion losses the marketing page forgets. Then the watt-hours meet the flight, where a labeled 100 Wh line sorts the bank into the bands the airlines talk about. Bank math does the arithmetic for all three. Exact numbers, every borrowed figure labeled.

The three tools

  • Watt-hour plan - the milliamp-hours and the labeled cell voltage become the watt-hours the label should have led with.
  • Charge plan - the milliamp-hours, after an honest delivery factor, become the charges your phone actually gets.
  • Flight plan - the watt-hours against the common cabin bands become the band the bank falls in.

Labeled norms

Labeled norms: a 3.7 V cell, a 65% honest delivery factor, and a 100 Wh common cabin band with an airline-approval band up to 160 Wh - all illustrative defaults. Where your device prints a watt-hour rating, trust that number over any estimate. Airlines set their own rules: many also limit how many banks you may carry and ban recharging one onboard, so check your carrier's current rules before the flight. A lithium bank never rides in checked luggage.
